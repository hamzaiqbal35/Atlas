## Overview

The Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB) is the remnant electromagnetic radiation from an early stage of the universe, often referred to as a "relic radiation." Filling all of space uniformly, it is the oldest light in the universe, dating to the epoch of recombination. Before this epoch, the universe was a hot, dense plasma of photons, electrons, and baryons. Photons were constantly scattered by free electrons via Thomson scattering, making the universe opaque. As the universe expanded and cooled to approximately $3000$ K, electrons and protons combined to form neutral hydrogen atoms, decoupling the radiation from matter. The photons then traveled freely through space, their wavelengths stretched by cosmic expansion into the microwave region of the electromagnetic spectrum observed today.

## Anisotropies and Power Spectrum

While the CMB is isotropic to roughly one part in $100,000$, minute temperature fluctuations ($\Delta T / T \sim 10^{-5}$) exist across the sky. These anisotropies are the imprints of quantum density fluctuations present in the very early universe, expanded to macroscopic scales during cosmic inflation.

The statistical properties of these fluctuations are best analyzed by expanding the temperature map in spherical harmonics ($Y_{\ell m}$):

$$
\frac{\Delta T}{T}(\theta, \phi) = \sum_{\ell=2}^{\infty} \sum_{m=-\ell}^{\ell} a_{\ell m} Y_{\ell m}(\theta, \phi)
$$

The angular power spectrum, plotting the variance $C_\ell = \langle |a_{\ell m}|^2 \rangle$ against multipole moment $\ell$, reveals acoustic peaks. These peaks correspond to baryon acoustic oscillations (BAO)—sound waves that propagated through the primordial photon-baryon fluid. The first acoustic peak ($\ell \approx 200$) provides a precise measurement of the spatial curvature of the universe, confirming it is extremely close to flat ($k=0$). Subsequent peaks yield precise values for the baryon density and cold dark matter density.

## Polarization and B-Modes

The CMB is also weakly polarized (at the microkelvin level) due to Thomson scattering of quadrupole temperature anisotropies at the surface of last scattering. This polarization is decomposed into curl-free E-modes (even parity) and divergence-free B-modes (odd parity). 

E-modes, first detected by DASI in 2002, arise naturally from scalar density perturbations. B-modes, however, can only be generated by primordial gravitational waves (tensor perturbations) or gravitational lensing of E-modes by massive galaxy clusters. Detecting primordial B-modes is a massive ongoing effort, as their amplitude (parameterized by the tensor-to-scalar ratio $r$) is a direct measure of the energy scale of cosmic inflation.

## Examples to Understand Concepts

- **The Fog Clearing:** Imagine standing in a dense, glowing fog where you can't see anything because light keeps bouncing off the water droplets. Suddenly, the fog condenses into rain and the air clears. The epoch of recombination is exactly this "clearing of the fog," where the plasma (fog) condensed into neutral atoms (rain), allowing the first light to travel straight to your eyes.
- **The Microwave Oven in Space:** If your eyes could see microwave radiation, the night sky would not be dark but rather glow uniformly in all directions with a bright, steady hum. A small percentage of the static on an untuned analog television set is actually from CMB photons crashing into the antenna.

## Recent Research & Discoveries

- **Planck Satellite Constraints:** The final data release from the ESA's Planck satellite mapped the CMB with unprecedented precision, tightly constraining the ΛCDM model parameters. It definitively established the universe's composition: $\sim 68\%$ dark energy, $\sim 27\%$ dark matter, and $\sim 5\%$ baryonic matter.
- **Sunyaev-Zel'dovich (SZ) Effect:** Modern high-resolution CMB surveys like the Atacama Cosmology Telescope (ACT) and the South Pole Telescope (SPT) use the SZ effect—where CMB photons are inverse-Compton scattered to higher energies by hot gas in galaxy clusters—to discover thousands of massive, high-redshift galaxy clusters, tracing the late-time evolution of large-scale structure.
- **Lensing of the CMB:** Recent high-precision mapping of how the CMB is gravitationally lensed by the intervening large-scale structure has provided an independent probe of the sum of neutrino masses ($\sum m_\nu$), placing stringent upper limits of $< 0.12$ eV.

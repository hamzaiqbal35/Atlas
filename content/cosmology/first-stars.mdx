The birth of the first stars, designated as **Population III** stars, marks the end of the Cosmic Dark Ages. Emerging from pristine clouds of primordial hydrogen and helium, these colossal stellar engines brought the first light to the cosmos since the fading of the Cosmic Microwave Background.

## The Jeans Mass and Collapse

During the Dark Ages, dark matter halos grew through gravitational accretion. Neutral gas fell into these potential wells. For a gas cloud to collapse and form a star, its internal gravity must overcome the outward thermal pressure of the gas. This threshold is known as the **Jeans Mass** ($M_J$):

$$
M_J \approx \left( \frac{5 k_B T}{G m} \right)^{3/2} \left( \frac{3}{4 \pi \rho} \right)^{1/2}
$$

Because the primordial gas lacked heavy elements (metals) to act as efficient coolants, it could not easily radiate away its thermal energy. The primary cooling mechanism was the formation of trace amounts of molecular hydrogen ($H_2$), which emits photons when collisionally excited. However, $H_2$ is a poor coolant compared to heavier elements like carbon or oxygen.

As a result, the gas clouds had to become extremely massive before their gravity could trigger a collapse. Consequently, the first stars were gargantuan.

## Properties of Population III Stars

Unlike modern stars (like our Sun) which contain a mix of heavy elements, Population III stars had **zero metallicity** ($Z=0$). 

Theoretical models suggest these stars were true behemoths, possessing masses between 100 and 1,000 times that of the Sun. Their immense mass led to incredibly high core temperatures and pressures, causing them to burn through their nuclear fuel at a ferocious rate.

Their lifespans were fleeting on a cosmic scale—lasting only a few million years. They shone with brilliant, intense ultraviolet light, millions of times more luminous than the Sun.

## The Epoch of Reionization

The intense UV radiation emitted by Population III stars began to strip electrons away from the surrounding neutral hydrogen gas, creating expanding bubbles of ionized plasma. As more stars and early galaxies formed, these bubbles grew and merged.

This process is known as the **Epoch of Reionization**. By roughly 1 billion years after the Big Bang, nearly all the hydrogen in the intergalactic medium had been reionized, rendering the universe transparent to UV light once again.

## Supernova and Seeding the Cosmos

When Population III stars reached the end of their brief lives, they exploded in spectacularly violent **pair-instability supernovae**. These explosions forged the very first heavy elements—carbon, oxygen, iron—and scattered them across the pristine intergalactic medium.

These newly forged elements enriched subsequent gas clouds, allowing them to cool more efficiently. This paved the way for the formation of smaller, longer-lived stars (Population II and Population I), planets, and eventually, the chemistry required for life.

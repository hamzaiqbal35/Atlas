Galaxy formation and evolution studies how a nearly uniform early universe transformed into the highly structured cosmos we observe today. It bridges the microscopic quantum fluctuations generated during inflation with the macroscopic astrophysical objects—stars, galaxies, and galaxy clusters.

## The Cold Dark Matter Paradigm

The current standard model of structure formation is rooted in **Cold Dark Matter (CDM)**. Dark matter, which interacts primarily via gravity and not electromagnetism, was able to begin collapsing into dense structures long before ordinary baryonic matter.

Because the dark matter was "cold" (moving slowly compared to the speed of light), it clumped together hierarchically in a **bottom-up** process. Small structures formed first, which then merged to form progressively larger structures, known as dark matter halos.

## Baryonic Infall and Cooling

After the Epoch of Recombination, when ordinary matter decoupled from radiation, it was finally free to fall into the gravitational potential wells created by the dark matter halos.

As the baryonic gas (mostly hydrogen and helium) fell in, it gained kinetic energy and heated up via shocks, reaching temperatures of millions of degrees. For this gas to form stars, it needed to cool and condense.

The cooling rate $\Lambda(T)$ depends on the gas density and temperature. If the cooling time $t_{\text{cool}}$ is shorter than the free-fall time $t_{\text{ff}}$ of the halo, the gas can efficiently shed its thermal energy (usually through bremsstrahlung or atomic line emission), condense into a dense central disk, and trigger rampant star formation.

## The Structure of a Galaxy

The interplay between the spinning, cooling gas and the dominant dark matter halo gives rise to the characteristic structures of galaxies:
1. **The Dark Matter Halo**: The massive, invisible scaffolding holding the galaxy together.
2. **The Galactic Disk**: Formed as the cooling gas conserves its angular momentum, flattening into a spinning disk where most star formation occurs.
3. **The Bulge and Supermassive Black Hole**: The dense central region, frequently harboring a supermassive black hole whose active galactic nucleus (AGN) feedback regulates further star formation.

## The Cosmic Web

On the grandest scales, galaxies are not scattered randomly. They trace out a vast, interconnected network known as the **Cosmic Web**. Galaxies congregate in massive **clusters**, which are connected by long, thread-like **filaments** of gas and dark matter, separated by immense, empty **voids**.

This large-scale structure is a direct, magnified imprint of the primordial quantum fluctuations from the inflationary epoch, stretched across billions of light-years.

The **Epoch of Recombination** marks a profound phase transition in the history of the universe. Occurring approximately 378,000 years after the Big Bang, this is the period when the expanding, cooling universe crossed a critical temperature threshold, allowing free electrons and protons to form neutral hydrogen atoms for the first time.

## The Primordial Plasma

Prior to recombination, the universe was filled with a hot, dense plasma of photons, electrons, protons, and alpha particles (helium nuclei). In this state, photons could not travel freely; they were constantly scattering off free electrons via **Thomson scattering**. 

The mean free path of a photon was extremely short. As a result, the universe was entirely opaque, filled with an impenetrable cosmic fog.

## The Saha Equation

As the universe expanded, its temperature dropped. The fractional ionization $X_e$ of the plasma as a function of temperature $T$ can be approximated by the **Saha equation**:

$$
\frac{X_e^2}{1 - X_e} = \frac{1}{n} \left( \frac{m_e k_B T}{2\pi \hbar^2} \right)^{3/2} e^{-\frac{E_I}{k_B T}}
$$

where:
- $E_I \approx 13.6 \text{ eV}$ is the ionization energy of hydrogen.
- $n$ is the total number density of baryons.
- $m_e$ is the electron mass.

One might expect recombination to occur when $k_B T \approx 13.6 \text{ eV}$ ($T \approx 160,000 \text{ K}$). However, because there are roughly a billion photons for every baryon in the universe, the high-energy tail of the Planck distribution contains enough energetic photons to keep hydrogen ionized until the temperature drops much lower, to approximately $3,000 \text{ K}$ ($k_B T \approx 0.26 \text{ eV}$).

## Photon Decoupling

As the temperature reached $3,000 \text{ K}$, the rate of recombination vastly outpaced ionization. The density of free electrons plummeted. Without free electrons to scatter off, the universe suddenly became transparent to radiation.

This event is known as **photon decoupling**. The photons present at that moment streamed freely through space and have been travelling across the universe ever since.

## The Cosmic Microwave Background

The photons released during decoupling form the **Cosmic Microwave Background (CMB)**. Originally emitted at $3,000 \text{ K}$ (a peak wavelength in the visible and near-infrared spectrum), cosmic expansion has stretched their wavelength by a factor of $z \approx 1089$. 

Today, this radiation is detected as a nearly perfect blackbody at a temperature of $2.725 \text{ K}$, peaking in the microwave region of the electromagnetic spectrum. The CMB represents the oldest light in the universe—a direct snapshot of the cosmos precisely at the moment of recombination.

## Overview
A neutron is a subatomic particle with no net electric charge and a mass slightly larger than that of a proton. Like the proton, it is a composite particle (a hadron) made of three quarks. Protons and neutrons constitute the nuclei of atoms. Since protons have a positive charge, they repel each other; neutrons act as the nuclear glue that stabilizes the nucleus.

## The Need for Neutrons
Without neutrons, the intense electromagnetic repulsion between positively charged protons would tear atomic nuclei apart. The strong nuclear force between neutrons and protons overcomes this repulsion, allowing heavy elements to exist. In a free state outside a nucleus, a neutron is unstable and decays into a proton, an electron, and an antineutrino in about 15 minutes.

## Examples to Understand Concepts
- **The Nuclear Peacemaker:** Imagine a room full of people who hate each other (protons). If you put them in a tight space, they will fight and push each other away. Neutrons are the neutral friends you place between them, holding their hands tightly enough (via the strong force) to keep everyone peacefully in the room.
- **Isotopes:** Adding a neutron to an atom doesn't change its chemical identity, but it changes its weight. Carbon-12 has 6 protons and 6 neutrons. Add two more neutrons, and you get Carbon-14—still carbon, but now heavy and radioactive.

## Recent Research & Discoveries
- Precision measurements of the "neutron lifetime" are critical for understanding how elements were forged in the first few minutes after the Big Bang. Curiously, two different methods for measuring this lifetime (the "bottle" method and the "beam" method) yield slightly different results, a discrepancy that remains unsolved.
- Free neutrons are widely used in materials science and biology for "neutron scattering," a technique to probe the internal structure of complex materials without damaging them.

## Overview
Dark matter is a hypothetical form of matter thought to account for approximately 85% of the matter in the universe and about a quarter of its total mass-energy density. It is called "dark" because it does not appear to interact with the electromagnetic field, meaning it does not absorb, reflect, or emit electromagnetic radiation and is therefore difficult to detect.

## The Evidence for Dark Matter
The primary evidence for dark matter comes from calculations showing that many galaxies would fly apart, or that they would not have formed or would not move as they do, if they did not contain a large amount of unseen matter. This was first notably observed by Vera Rubin in the 1970s when studying the rotation curves of spiral galaxies.

## Examples to Understand Concepts
- **The Merry-Go-Round Analogy:** Imagine a merry-go-round spinning incredibly fast. The children on the outer edge should fly off unless they are strapped in with strong, invisible seatbelts. Dark matter acts as the gravitational "seatbelt" keeping fast-spinning galaxies intact.
- **Gravitational Lensing:** Just as a glass lens bends light, the immense gravity of dark matter clusters bends the light from distant background galaxies, distorting their images into arcs and rings.

## Recent Research & Discoveries
- Highly sensitive underground detectors, like LUX-ZEPLIN (LZ) and XENONnT, are currently searching for direct interactions between dark matter particles (like WIMPs) and regular atoms.
- Some researchers are exploring alternative theories to dark matter, such as Modified Newtonian Dynamics (MOND), though dark matter remains the consensus model in astrophysics.

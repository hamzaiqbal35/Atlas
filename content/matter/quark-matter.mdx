## Overview
Quark matter, often existing in the state of a quark-gluon plasma (QGP), is an extreme phase of quantum chromodynamics (QCD). Under normal conditions, quarks and gluons are permanently trapped within hadrons (like protons and neutrons) due to color confinement. However, under extraordinarily high temperatures and densities, they break free, forming a "soup" of freely roaming quarks and gluons.

## The Big Bang and Core Collapses
For a few millionths of a second after the Big Bang, the entire universe was filled with a super-hot quark-gluon plasma before it expanded and cooled enough for protons and neutrons to condense. Today, the only natural place where quark matter might exist in a stable form is deep within the cores of the most massive neutron stars.

## Examples to Understand Concepts
- **The Melting Ice Analogy:** If you heat ice, it melts into liquid water, freeing the previously locked H2O molecules to flow. If you heat a proton to trillions of degrees, the "ice" of the proton melts, and its constituent quarks and gluons flow freely as a liquid plasma.
- **The Perfect Fluid:** Experiments have shown that quark-gluon plasma behaves like a nearly "perfect fluid" with almost zero viscosity, meaning it flows with no internal friction or resistance, unlike any substance on Earth.

## Recent Research & Discoveries
- Heavy-ion collisions at the Relativistic Heavy Ion Collider (RHIC) and the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) routinely recreate microscopic droplets of quark-gluon plasma that exist for a fraction of a second, allowing scientists to study the universe's original state.
- Astrophysicists are searching for observational signatures of "quark stars"—theoretical stellar remnants composed entirely of quark matter.
